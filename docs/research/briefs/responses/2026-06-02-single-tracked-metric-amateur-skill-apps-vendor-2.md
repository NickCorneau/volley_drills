# Single Self-Tracked Metric for Adherence and Skill Improvement in Amateur Skill-Sport Athletes

## Executive view

The strongest evidence-supported conclusion is that **a single weekly metric is probably not honest enough to carry both adherence and skill-improvement signal** for self-coached adult amateur skill-sport athletes training only one to three times per week. The direct literature for this exact population is thin; the usable evidence comes from four adjacent literatures instead: physical-activity adherence, rehabilitation exercise adherence, athlete monitoring, and motor learning / deliberate practice. Taken together, those literatures point to **goal attainment and practice exposure** as the most reliable adherence-side signals, and to **practice quality plus skill-specific confidence or task performance** as the most plausible improvement-side signals. That makes a **two-metric weekly readout** more defensible than any single metric. citeturn27search0turn27search3turn39view0turn40view0turn33search0turn34search0

If the product must ship **one** user-facing weekly number, the best choice is **planned-vs-completed session ratio**. It has the cleanest connection to long-horizon adherence because early goal attainment predicts later goal adherence, and because planning variables meaningfully explain later exercise behavior. It also preserves comparability across people training at different weekly doses, which raw session count does not. Its weakness is that it is only an **indirect** improvement signal: it tells you whether practice happened as intended, not whether the practiced skill improved. citeturn27search0turn27search3turn39view0turn36view0turn38view0

If the product can ship **two** numbers, the most defensible pair is **planned-vs-completed session ratio** as the load/adherence metric plus **weekly skill confidence or self-efficacy** as the skill proxy, with one important caveat: confidence is supported as a predictor of performance expression, not as a clean objective measure of learning. In other words, it is the best low-burden skill-side candidate from the listed menu, but it is still second-best to an objective repeated drill score if the product can support one later. citeturn33search0turn33search2turn34search0turn17search0turn17search6

Confidence in the ranking is **moderate** for adherence, **low-to-moderate** for improvement, and **low** for exact population fit, because most studies are not on self-coached adult recreational volleyball/tennis/golf/climbing style users training one to three times per week. That gap is not a minor footnote; it is the core research fact. citeturn39view0turn36view0turn11view0turn40view0

## What the evidence actually says

The most robust improvement-side evidence favors **practice exposure**, not sophisticated load accounting. In a meta-analysis of 34 sports studies comprising 52 independent samples, 63 effect sizes, and 2,765 participants, accumulated deliberate practice correlated **r = .43** with sports performance, explaining about **18% of variance** and leaving most variance unexplained. That is not overwhelming, but it is materially stronger than the evidence behind most consumer-facing skill metrics. The implication is blunt: for amateur skill-sport users, simply getting the right amount of purposeful practice done remains the strongest documented behavioral route to improvement. citeturn40view0

The best adherence-side evidence favors **goal attainment and planning**. In a 12-month mHealth weight-loss trial of **502 adults**, the single strongest predictor of long-term physical-activity goal adherence was **first-week adherence** to the goal. In a six-month online program with **212 adults**, weekly adherence states for step and minute goals deteriorated early, and the authors explicitly identified the first two months as the main window for intervention. In three rehabilitation cohorts (**N = 353, 114, and 368**), planning and recovery self-efficacy jointly explained **14% to 39%** of later physical-activity variance, which is materially better than intuition-only product design. These are not skill-sport samples, but they are directly relevant to the adherence half of the question. citeturn27search0turn27search3turn39view0turn36view0turn38view0turn37view0

That same evidence base also explains why **planned-vs-completed ratio** edges out **raw session count** as the best single metric. Count is simpler, but ratio handles dose heterogeneity: a golfer trying to practice once this week and a climber trying to practice three times can both score 1.0 if they hit plan. For a product serving one-to-three-sessions-per-week users, that normalization matters. The downside is that ratio can flatter a low plan. The practical fix is simple: use ratio as the user-facing metric and keep raw count in the backend. citeturn27search0turn39view0turn36view0

The best evidence for a **subjective skill proxy** is the confidence / self-efficacy literature. A 2022 meta-analysis of state self-confidence and sport performance reported **r = .24**, accounting for about **5.8% shared variance**. A 2023 meta-analysis of pre-event self-efficacy and sports performance reported **r = .31**. Those effects are not large enough to justify replacing behavioral metrics, but they are good enough to justify using a one-item confidence measure as the skill-side half of a minimal weekly pair. The catch is that confidence mostly tracks **readiness to express skill**, not clean objective learning. citeturn34search0turn34search1turn33search0turn33search2

The evidence against making **sRPE** the main weekly number is not that sRPE is bad; it is that sRPE solves a somewhat different problem. Reviews of session-RPE show it is a valid and reliable internal-load measure across many sports and age groups. But the underlying use case is usually load management in coached or higher-volume settings. In the adjacent literature on single-item wellness monitoring, relationships with training load are often **trivial to moderate**, and the systematic review explicitly warns that links between subjective single-item measures and objective load may be unclear or not especially useful. For a low-frequency amateur skill user, sRPE is defensible as backend telemetry and weak as the main weekly story. citeturn30search0turn30search12turn45search0turn45search1

The evidence for **focused-skill counts** and **drill completion** is noticeably thinner than their face validity suggests. A six-week progressive-practice study in healthy adults found that progressive challenge produced better motor performance than non-progressive practice, with significant between-group differences on harder task levels after training and at short retention. That finding supports the underlying idea that **specific, progressively challenging practice** matters. What it does **not** support is the simpler claim that self-counted contacts or completed drills are themselves reliable weekly predictors. Quality of challenge matters more than rote completion. citeturn42view0

## Failure modes and noise floors

The most important implementation warning is that **more measurement is not automatically better**. In a ten-week study of **35 recreational athletes** asked to complete daily training monitoring in an app, adherence was only **43.0%** in the control arm and **45.8%** in the education arm; education improved perceived understanding but did not improve adherence. In a related implementation study across **131 athletes**, interest did not translate cleanly into usage, and data output plus low burden were major determinants of continued engagement. In qualitative work with **30 stakeholders across 20 sports programs**, effective self-report systems were described as requiring minimal burden and strong social support. For your use case, that argues aggressively against within-session drill logging as a required weekly receipt input. citeturn11view0turn12view0turn19search1

**Streaks** are the clearest example of a metric that can help and then backfire. In a Journal of Consumer Research paper spanning **seven studies**, intact streaks increased subsequent engagement relative to broken streaks. Duolingo’s own product writing is revealing: the company says streaks are highly motivating, but it also added Streak Freezes and later allowed up to two at a time; that change increased the relative number of daily active learners by **0.38%**. The practitioner lesson is obvious. Streaks work best when the system actively softens the cost of an inevitable miss. A weekly sports app that makes streak length the central receipt without recovery mechanics is asking for “ghosting after a broken streak.” citeturn21search0turn21search1turn46search0turn46search1

**Confidence prompts** carry a different risk. In a 16-week study of **335 athletes**, self-report monitoring was associated with higher confidence and extrinsic motivation over time, but also with lower satisfaction and intrinsic motivation early on; novice athletes and athletes using the tool less autonomously were less responsive. That means a weekly confidence item is viable, but it should be presented gently and interpreted carefully. It is a skill-side proxy, not a moral score. citeturn17search0turn17search6

The exact **noise floors** for your exact metrics and population are mostly not published, so any hard threshold would be fake precision. Still, the literature gives three practical boundaries. First, one to three sessions per week is probably too sparse for fast habit formation: in new gym members (**N = 111**), the minimum requirement to establish an exercise habit was about **four bouts per week for six weeks**, and consistency was the strongest longitudinal predictor of habit growth (**b = .21**). Second, week-to-week goal adherence is fragile early and should be interpreted over rolling windows, not from one week alone. Third, individual learning deltas are noisy: in a large crowdsourced motor-learning study, early performance strongly predicted later performance rank, but it explained almost none of the variance in **learning itself**, and the authors argued that repeated measures are needed because difference scores are unreliable. That combination strongly suggests that a weekly product surface should avoid pretending that tiny week-to-week movements are precise. citeturn25view0turn39view0turn43view0

## Recommended weekly readout

For the product decision embedded in the brief, the most defensible answer is:

**Use a two-metric pair, not one metric, if you can.**

The recommended pair is **planned-vs-completed session ratio** plus **weekly skill confidence / self-efficacy**. The first metric is the best-supported adherence signal in a low-burden weekly format. The second is the lowest-burden candidate that has a real, meta-analytic relationship with sport performance. This pair also cleanly separates the two messages the app is trying to send: “you kept showing up” and “your skill loop feels stronger.” Those are not the same construct, and the literature says the product should stop pretending they are. citeturn27search0turn39view0turn36view0turn33search0turn34search0

If you absolutely must ship **one** metric, use **planned-vs-completed session ratio**. It wins because it is the least misleading compromise. It is lower burden than drill-level instrumentation, more individualized than simple count, and better aligned with adherence prediction than sRPE or streak mechanics. Do **not** claim that it directly measures improvement; claim only that it tracks whether the user is sustaining the practice plan most likely to support improvement. citeturn27search0turn39view0turn11view0turn30search0

If you can expand the candidate set later, the best future upgrade is not another wellness item. It is a **single repeated structured drill score** for the focal weakness: the same task, same scoring rule, same band of difficulty, measured often enough to beat noise. The motor-learning literature and skill-app practice intuition both point that way. Yousician’s public product language, for example, emphasizes **instant feedback on precision and timing**, rewards, and weekly activity reporting rather than a single generic intensity metric; Duolingo’s public writing shows the opposite half of the design problem, where streaks need forgiveness mechanics to avoid becoming punitive. The broader practitioner pattern is clear: consumer skill apps tend to pair an adherence loop with a skill-feedback loop rather than forcing one number to do both jobs. citeturn42view0turn43view0turn47search2turn47search8turn46search0turn46search1

## Open questions and limitations

The biggest limitation is not methodological nitpicking. It is that **the direct target-population literature is missing**. I did not find a peer-reviewed evidence base built specifically around self-coached adult amateur skill-sport athletes practicing one to three times per week and relating a single self-tracked weekly metric to both 8–12 week adherence and objective skill gain. Most of the usable evidence comes from obesity treatment, rehabilitation, elite or team-sport monitoring, and general motor-learning experiments. That means the ranking above is evidence-based, but still partly extrapolative. citeturn27search0turn39view0turn36view0turn11view0turn40view0

Three unanswered questions matter enough that they should shape product humility. First, no strong study establishes whether **ratio** beats **count** for objective skill gains in low-dose recreational skill sports. Second, the literature supports **confidence** as a performance correlate, but not as a clean stand-in for **learning**. Third, I did not find hard published thresholds that say, for example, exactly how many drill reps or how much week-to-week sRPE change are needed before the signal rises above noise for this population. Until that exists, any weekly surface should emphasize trends over judgment and retain a clear distinction between **behavior completed** and **skill improved**. citeturn33search0turn34search0turn43view0turn45search1
