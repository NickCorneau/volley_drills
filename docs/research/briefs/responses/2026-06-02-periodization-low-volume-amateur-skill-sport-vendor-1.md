# Research Memo: Does Periodization Carry Signal for 1–3×/Week Adult Skill-Sport Amateurs?

## Executive Summary

**Headline verdict: MIXED, leaning toward "below the noise floor for measurable physical/skill effect, but real for adherence."** Confidence: **moderate-to-high on the physical/skill null, moderate on the adherence value, high on the "target-population literature is largely absent" finding.**

For the specific population in scope — adult recreational athletes, 2–5 years' experience, training 1–3 sessions/week, self-coached, in skill-dominant sports — there is **no defensible body of evidence that any classical periodization shape (linear/Bompa, block/Issurin, undulating/DUP, conjugate) produces a measurable advantage over simple progressive overload for *skill* outcomes.** The periodization evidence base is almost entirely about strength and power, mostly in younger, higher-frequency, or coached lifters; and even there the between-model differences are small. The motor-learning mechanisms most relevant to skill sports — contextual interference and distributed practice — tell a sharper story: (a) distributing practice across days/weeks robustly helps retention, but (b) the contextual-interference (random-practice) benefit shrinks to near-zero in applied sport settings and in novices, and can reverse at low practice doses.

**The single most important caveat:** The target population is systematically excluded from the periodization literature. A dedicated evidence search confirmed that **no randomized controlled trial tests multi-week periodization versus non-periodization for *technical skill* outcomes in adult recreational athletes at 1–3 sessions/week.** Periodization RCTs measure strength/power; the skill literature is contextual interference, largely in students, children, or lab tasks. Every periodization verdict below therefore rests on extrapolation, and that extrapolation should make the team humble, not confident.

**Decision implication:** Do **not** build a multi-week structural layer to capture a *physiological* periodization effect — that effect is below the noise floor at this volume, where neither fatigue nor recovery is the binding constraint. **Do** consider building a lightweight multi-week layer if and only if it is justified on **adherence/behavioral** grounds (goal-gradient, milestone framing, plan continuity, distributed-practice scheduling), and design it accordingly: as a motivational scaffold and a spacing engine, not a supercompensation engine. The right minimum-viable structure for a skill-sport amateur is qualitatively different from the strength/endurance amateur's: it should keep *all* core skills "alive" every week (decay-driven), not concentrate one quality per block (load-driven).

## Minimum-Effective Block-Length Section

Block periodization depends on **concentrated training load** producing an adaptation, plus a **residual training effect** that preserves it while the next quality is trained. Issurin's blocks run ~2–4 weeks and presuppose the high-volume loading of elite athletes. At 1–3 sessions/week, total weekly stimulus is too low to create the load concentration the residual-effect model requires. There is **no published evidence of a minimum block length producing a measurable cumulative training effect at this volume** in skill sports.

What *is* well-supported is the **distributed-practice / spacing** literature: spacing practice across days rather than massing it within a single day improves long-term retention (Shea et al., *Human Movement Science*; surgical-skill RCTs find weekly and monthly distribution both work, with distributed > massed; Dail & Christina 2004 show distributed > massed for novice golf putting at acquisition *and* retention out to 28 days). This is a **spacing** finding, not a **block** finding.

**Below the elite-volume floor, "block periodization" is a misnomer — what remains is progressive overload plus sensible spacing of sessions.** For product purposes, the meaningful structural unit is therefore not a physiological "block" but a **skill-focus rotation** plus a **spacing schedule**. If a structural unit is shipped, the smallest defensible one is a **3–6 week skill-focus cycle**: long enough to give a theme and a visible arc, short enough to revisit skills before they decay, and matched to the 6–12 week windows over which skill interventions in this space actually demonstrate learning (see Skill-Consolidation section).

## Contextual-Interference-at-Low-Volume Section

This is the most decision-relevant motor-learning finding, and it cuts **against** aggressive practice randomization for this population.

- **Lab vs. applied gap.** Brady's (2004, *Perceptual and Motor Skills* 99:116–126) meta-analysis of 61 studies (139 effect-size estimates) found an average overall effect of d=0.38, but the effect for **basic/lab research was d=0.57 versus only d=0.19 for applied (sport) research** — and was d=0.50 in adults versus a negligible d=0.10 in younger learners.
- **Largest recent meta-analysis is skeptical.** Ammar et al. (2024, *Educational Psychology Review* 36:57; senior author Schöllhorn) screened 933 records, selected 36 studies, pooled 183 outcomes, and found that **only 37 of 183 outcomes (20%) agreed with the predicted paradoxical CI effect.** The pre-post difference between blocked and random was non-significant (ES=0.1, p=0.35); the retention advantage for high CI was statistically detectable but too small to produce a greater long-term gain (ES≈−0.13, p=0.18). The authors explicitly question generalizing CI to physical-education and sport practice.
- **"The myth" meta-analysis.** Ammar et al. (2023, *Educational Research Review*) concluded the CI benefit in sport practice is largely a "myth," with age, experience, sport, and skill type all failing to moderate the blocked-vs-random difference.
- **Dose threshold / sign reversal.** Verwey, Wright & Immink (2022, *Psychological Research* 86:1310–1331) showed in healthy adults that the random-practice benefit appears only with short sequences receiving *limited* practice and **reverses** (blocked > random) under *extended* practice — direct evidence of a dose-dependent sign flip.
- **Counter-evidence (flag the dispute).** Czyż et al. (2024, *Scientific Reports* 14:15974) found high CI *does* improve retention overall, but even they report the benefit is "almost negligible" in applied settings and negligible/non-significant in young participants. The Ammar and Czyż groups are in open methodological dispute; present both.

**Implication:** For a 2–5-year recreational player practicing 1–3×/week, heavy random/interleaved scheduling is not reliably beneficial and may impair early acquisition of a *new* skill. The defensible design is **blocked-leaning practice when introducing a genuinely new skill, shifting to interleaved/varied (constraint-led) practice once basic competence exists.** Variability should itself be periodized at the skill level — not imposed uniformly.

## Skill-Consolidation Evidence Section

The volleyball-coaching anchor "~3 months at 2–3 sessions/week to consolidate a new skill" is a **practitioner heuristic, not an empirically validated threshold.** Williams & Hodges (2023, *Journal of Sports Sciences*) state explicitly that there is a general lack of evidence on the relationship between sport-skill practice and long-term retention/forgetting, and that consolidation research has been restricted to lab tasks (manual aiming, sequencing).

What the broader literature supports:
- **Consolidation happens offline**, over hours to days, with sleep playing a major role — favoring distributed sessions over massed.
- **Real sport-skill interventions that show learning typically run 6–12 weeks at 2–3 sessions/week** — e.g., 8-week volleyball-serve contextual-interference teaching experiments, 12-week novice tennis programs, and 6-week, 2×/week skill studies. This is *consistent with* the "~3 months" anchor as an order-of-magnitude figure, but no study identifies a minimum.
- **Distributed > massed for sport skills**: novice golf-putting retention is better with practice distributed across days than massed (Dail & Christina 2004, *RQES* 75:148–155).

**Verdict on the anchor:** Corroborated in magnitude (months, not weeks; multiple sessions/week), but **not** as a precise consolidation constant. The app should treat "expect roughly 8–12 weeks of repeated, spaced exposure to consolidate a new skill" as a reasonable planning assumption, explicitly flagged as a heuristic rather than a measured law.

## Periodization-as-Adherence-Mechanism Section *(kept separate from physical-mechanism evidence)*

This is where a multi-week layer can be justified — on **behavioral, not physiological** grounds, though the evidence is indirect (drawn from behavior-change and UX literature, not skill-sport RCTs).

- **Goal-gradient effect.** People increase effort as they perceive themselves approaching a goal (Hull 1932). The canonical human demonstration is Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng (2006, *Journal of Marketing Research* 43:39–58): café customers buy coffee more frequently the closer they are to a free reward, and an "endowed-progress" card (12 stamps with 2 pre-filled) accelerates purchases versus a 10-stamp card. A visible multi-week arc with a milestone/"peak" supplies exactly the proximity signal this effect rewards.
- **Behavior-change techniques (BCTs).** Adherence meta-analyses find goal-setting, action planning, self-monitoring, and feedback produce small but real adherence improvements (panoramic meta-analysis of physiotherapy adherence: SMD≈0.24, I²=0%). A multi-week plan is a vehicle for several of these BCTs simultaneously.
- **Flexible goal structure beats rigid.** App-design evidence suggests structure that adapts to the user outperforms fixed daily prescriptions — consistent with the app's existing autoregulation. (Reported industry examples, e.g., flexible weekly-goal features improving 90-day retention, should be treated as vendor-reported, not peer-reviewed.)
- **Friction caveat.** Progress structures can also create pressure and drop-off; rigid plans that users fall behind on can drive abandonment. The adherence value is real but **conditional on the structure being flexible and forgiving.**

**Conclusion:** A multi-week layer is defensible primarily as an **adherence and spacing scaffold** — it gives the recreational athlete a sense of progression, a milestone to anticipate, and a reason to return — independent of any physical periodization effect. This is the *only* mechanism with positive (if indirect) evidence for the target population.

## Recommended Minimum-Viable Block Structure

**Recommendation: Ship a *lightweight, optional, adherence-first* multi-week layer — not a physiologically-justified periodization engine.** If built, the minimum-viable structure is:

- **Block length:** **4-week skill-focus cycles** (acceptable range 3–6 weeks). Long enough for a theme and a visible arc; short enough to revisit skills before decay. Justified as a milestone cadence and a spacing window, *not* as a supercompensation block.
- **Block sequence:** **Theme rotation, not quality periodization.** E.g., Cycle 1 "serve & serve-receive," Cycle 2 "defense & transition," Cycle 3 "attacking" — with *every* core skill kept present at a maintenance level in every cycle (conjugate-like maintenance, because skill decays without touch). This matches what strong low-frequency coaches already do.
- **Intra-block organization:** **Blocked-leaning practice when introducing a *new* skill; transition to interleaved/varied (constraint-led) practice once basic competence is shown.** Periodize *variability itself* at the skill level. This is the direct product translation of the contextual-interference evidence.
- **Spacing:** **Enforce distribution of each skill's exposure across the week** rather than massing it — the one robustly evidence-backed scheduling lever (distributed > massed for retention).
- **Re-test / progression triggers:** Re-test a skill at the end of each cycle (every 3–4 weeks) with a simple, repeatable skill check; advance difficulty when completion *and* self-rated quality cross a threshold. This rides the existing perceived-effort/completion signals rather than a rigid calendar — staying inside the autoregulation paradigm the evidence favors.

**Cheapest defensible alternative:** Ship **no** multi-week block model. Instead, surface a "skill focus for the next few weeks" suggestion plus distributed scheduling and milestone re-tests layered on top of the existing autoregulation. This captures the adherence and spacing benefits at a fraction of the build/maintenance cost. Given the absence of physical-mechanism evidence, **this is the recommended starting point;** graduate to the fuller 4-week-cycle structure only if A/B data show the lighter version drives the adherence lift.

**Thresholds that would change this recommendation:** (1) If an A/B test shows the multi-week layer materially lifts 8–12-week retention/adherence, invest further. (2) If it shows no adherence lift or increased drop-off (the "friction" risk), remove it. (3) If credible skill-sport, low-volume periodization RCTs emerge showing a skill effect, revisit the physical-mechanism justification.

## Practitioner Intuition Section *(separated from peer-reviewed evidence)*

Applied coaches in amateur skill-sports converge on some points that align with the evidence and diverge on others:

- **Volleyball (CoachingVB / The Art of Coaching).** At 2–3 sessions/week, coaches insist every core skill (serve, pass) appear in *every* practice — *"I would not want my players going 3–4 days without serving and passing if I can avoid it."* This is explicitly anti-block intuition and aligns with the evidence that skill decays without frequent touch and that long single-quality blocks are inappropriate at low volume.
- **Climbing (Eva López; TrainingBeta; Climbing.com / Consuegra).** Practitioners recommend linear periodization for *beginners* and reserve undulating/block for *advanced* climbers who have stopped progressing — converging with strength evidence that periodization model matters more as training age rises. Consuegra cites that a plan beats no plan by roughly 1.5–2.3% (Muñoz 2017), a small effect consistent with the literature.
- **Tennis / racquet S&C (Athletic Performance Academy).** Coaches use DUP for the *physical* (strength/power) side of tennis, not for stroke skill — mirroring the physical/skill distinction this memo enforces.
- **Where intuition diverges from evidence.** Much practitioner writing (especially in climbing) assumes supercompensation and residual training effects apply to recreational athletes. The evidence says these are largely elite-volume phenomena; practitioners over-borrow from high-performance frameworks. Where coaches keep all skills "alive" weekly and individualize by feel, they converge with the evidence; where they sell 12-week peaking macrocycles to weekend players, they diverge from it.

## Gaps Section

The literature does **not** answer the core question. Specifically:

1. **No RCT** compares periodized vs. non-periodized **skill** training in adult recreational athletes at 1–3 sessions/week. Periodization RCTs measure strength/power; the skill literature is contextual interference and largely uses students, children, or lab tasks. A targeted search for block/linear/DUP periodization trials with *technical-skill* outcomes in adult amateurs returned **none**.
2. **No validated consolidation constant** for sport skills in adults at low frequency — the "~3 months" anchor is a heuristic; consolidation research is confined to lab tasks (Williams & Hodges 2023 explicitly note this).
3. **The contextual-interference dispute is unresolved** (Ammar "myth" papers vs. Czyż 2024 *Scientific Reports*), with active methodological disagreement.

**Study design to close the gap:** A 12-week, 3-arm RCT in adult recreational players (2–5 yrs' experience, training ~2×/week, self-directed via the app): **(A)** simple progressive overload / constant skill focus; **(B)** 4-week skill-focus cycles with maintenance of other skills; **(C)** autoregulated readiness-driven. Primary outcome: a blinded sport-specific skill test at baseline, 6 weeks, 12 weeks, and a 6-week retention test. Secondary outcomes: adherence (sessions completed), self-rated progression, dropout. Powering for a between-arm skill effect of d≈0.4 needs roughly 60–70 participants per arm. This would directly test whether the multi-week layer beats both the simpler and the more adaptive alternatives on skill *and* adherence.

**The absence itself is decision-grade.** Because target-population evidence for a *physical* effect is essentially missing, any product choice in this layer is on speculative ground physiologically. The team should default to the simpler, cheaper structure and justify the layer on adherence — then validate with its own A/B data, which would be the most authoritative evidence in existence for this exact population.
