# **Research brief: Construct validity of weekly self-rated skill confidence as a longitudinal skill-development proxy in adult recreational athletes**

## **Executive Summary**

The assessment of skill acquisition in adult recreational athletes outside of clinical or expert-supervised environments presents a formidable psychometric challenge. A central design and product question is whether a weekly, single-item, self-rated skill confidence score possesses sufficient construct validity to serve as a user-facing longitudinal proxy for actual skill development. The target demographic comprises self-coached adult recreational athletes with two to five years of experience in technical skill-sports (e.g., tennis, golf, climbing, volleyball) training one to three sessions per week. An exhaustive synthesis of the psychometric, sport psychology, and human-computer interaction (HCI) literature indicates that weekly self-rated skill confidence carries acceptable, though highly conditional, construct validity. It can be successfully deployed, provided its architectural implementation explicitly mitigates severe known psychological confounds.  
The evidence demonstrates that single-item subjective measures can be both highly reliable and predictive of longitudinal behavior, operating on par with multi-item inventories when properly constrained.1 However, weekly self-rated confidence does not map onto actual physical skill development in a linear, one-to-one ratio. Instead, it operates through a heavily mediated cognitive pathway subjected to severe short-horizon distortions. Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) studies indicate that within-person variance in state self-efficacy ranges from 51% to 89%, proving that self-reported confidence fluctuates dramatically based on daily environmental and physiological factors rather than underlying mechanical changes in motor pathways.4 Furthermore, self-rated confidence is highly vulnerable to recency bias, immediate past-session outcomes, acute physical fatigue, and transient negative mood states.6  
Perhaps the most critical threat to construct validity in this specific population—adult recreational athletes with two to five years of experience—is the decoupling of actual competence and perceived competence due to metacognitive shifts. The Dunning-Kruger effect causes novices to drastically overestimate their abilities, as they lack the conceptual framework required to recognize their own incompetence.9 As athletes transition into the intermediate phase, they frequently encounter the "paradox of progress." During this phase of motor learning, actual mechanical skill improvements generate heightened metacognitive awareness. This paradoxically suppresses self-rated confidence because the athlete becomes acutely aware of their technical limitations.11 Consequently, intermediate athletes often experience subjective "plateau-blindness," where confidence traces flatline or even decline despite objective, underlying improvements in motor learning.14  
Despite these formidable confounds, self-rated confidence remains a valuable longitudinal proxy if engineered correctly. The recommended approach is to deploy a 100-point Visual Analog Scale (VAS) or an 11-point Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) anchored around specific capability expectations rather than retrospective performance evaluations. The item wording must adhere strictly to established guidelines for self-efficacy construction, utilizing "can do" capability framing (e.g., "How confident are you in your capability to execute successfully today?") rather than comparative or retrospective phrasing.16  
Compared to purely objective behavioral proxies like session counts or drill-completion rates, subjective confidence captures an entirely different dimension of the athlete's psychological state—one that directly predicts future engagement, psychological resilience, and persistence in the face of failure.2 The optimal application design should therefore utilize the subjective confidence trace not as an absolute, flawless measure of physical mastery, but as an indicator of self-efficacy and user engagement, ideally paired alongside a behavioral proxy to triangulate true skill acquisition and identify periods of plateau-blindness.

## **Conceptual Framework: Distinguishing State Confidence, Trait Confidence, and Self-Efficacy**

To accurately evaluate the construct validity of a weekly confidence readout within a digital application, it is first necessary to parse the specific psychological constructs being measured. The terminology surrounding self-evaluation is often conflated in colloquial use, but the literature in sport psychology historically divides perceived capability into three distinct, though interrelated, constructs: trait sport-confidence, state sport-confidence, and self-efficacy.  
Self-efficacy, originally formulated by Albert Bandura in 1977 and continuously refined over subsequent decades, refers specifically to the strength of an individual's belief in their capability to organize and execute the specific courses of action required to produce a given outcome.18 It is heavily situation-specific and task-specific. Trait sport-confidence, by contrast, is a more global, dispositional belief in one's general athletic ability, remaining relatively stable over time across multiple contexts.22 State sport-confidence reflects an individual's certainty in their abilities at a specific, fleeting moment in time, often measured immediately prior to a competitive event or a high-stakes performance.22  
A weekly, single-item self-report administered via a mobile app inherently captures a localized metric most accurately described as state self-efficacy rather than trait confidence. When an adult amateur athlete evaluates their confidence on a Tuesday evening after a practice session, they are synthesizing highly contextual, recent information regarding their specific mechanical capabilities. Research indicates that while trait confidence serves as a baseline predictor for state confidence, state measures are significantly more volatile and much more closely linked to immediate behavioral outcomes. In a study examining high school athletes, trait sport-confidence predicted state sport-confidence, but only the state measures and specific self-efficacy judgments significantly predicted actual performance variance.23  
The construction of self-efficacy relies on four primary informational sources: enactive mastery experiences (the outcomes of past physical attempts), vicarious experiences (observing others), verbal persuasion (coaching and feedback), and physiological or affective states (arousal, fatigue, mood).18 For the target population of self-coached amateurs training one to three times weekly, this four-pillar framework is structurally compromised. Because these athletes are entirely self-coached and operate outside of structured team environments, they lack access to systematic verbal persuasion (expert coaching feedback) and structured vicarious experiences (curated observation of peers). As a result, their weekly confidence readings become heavily over-indexed on the remaining two sources: the visceral memory of their most recent enactive mastery experience and their current physiological state.  
Thus, while a weekly subjective trace successfully captures fluctuations in state self-efficacy, it requires careful interpretation by the system architecture. The metric is not measuring the steady, linear, underlying accumulation of physical motor pathways—which would represent trait mastery. Rather, it measures the athlete's fluctuating, highly reactive cognitive appraisal of those pathways on any given day. Recognizing this distinction is the foundational step in determining the construct validity of the weekly readout.

## **Psychometric Viability and Test-Retest Reliability of Single-Item Measures**

A primary methodological concern when deploying a one-tap, single-item assessment in a mobile application is the assumption that single-item measures inherently lack the psychometric rigor and validity of comprehensive, multi-item inventories. Within traditional psychometrics, single items are frequently viewed as suspect because traditional internal consistency statistics (such as Cronbach's alpha) cannot be mathematically computed for a single data point, and individual items are theoretically more vulnerable to random measurement error or idiosyncratic interpretations of vocabulary.1 However, the modern measurement science literature reveals robust empirical support for the use of single-item measures, particularly when evaluating global, unidimensional constructs like overall skill confidence or specific state self-efficacy.  
Empirical evidence demonstrates that single-item self-efficacy measures hold acceptable test-retest reliability, excellent convergent validity, and profound predictive validity across diverse behavioral domains. In clinical populations, a study of inpatient young adults (N=303) found that a single-item measure of self-efficacy consistently correlated positively with a well-established 20-item measure of self-efficacy, demonstrating high convergent validity.1 More importantly, the single-item measure successfully predicted long-term behavioral outcomes at one, three, and six months post-discharge, operating just as effectively as the cumbersome global subscale scores of the 20-item variant.1  
Within physical activity and sport contexts, the validation of single items is equally compelling. A longitudinal analysis examining the efficacy of personalized physical activity goals utilizing a mobile application found that weekly, real-time, single-item self-efficacy assessments successfully predicted subsequent behavioral adherence in a cohort of women (N=140). Participants who reported being "confident" in a single-item weekly measure were 4.41 times more likely to achieve their objectively measured behavioral goals in the subsequent week.2 This indicates that single-item tracking carries profound predictive validity regarding user motivation and future action.  
In the realm of collective sport efficacy, single-item measures have exhibited strong correlations with complex, multi-item inventories such as the Collective Efficacy Questionnaire for Sports (CEQS). Test-retest reliability across weekly and pre/post-intervention timelines yielded large, highly significant intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) ranging from 0.87 to 0.88 for single-item measures.3 Similarly, an evaluation of a newly proposed single-item general self-efficacy scale across an adult population (N=231) demonstrated satisfactory criterion-related validity, exhibiting a strong correlation (r \= 0.795) when compared directly to established multi-item counterparts.26  
The implication for a local-first mobile training application targeting adult recreational athletes is clear: a single-item subjective confidence measure is entirely psychometrically defensible and can yield highly reliable longitudinal data, provided the underlying construct being measured is narrow and universally understood by the respondent. If the skill being evaluated is distinctly bound and specific (e.g., "confidence in executing a topspin serve" or "confidence in bouldering dynamic overhangs"), a single item performs adequately without requiring the user to navigate an exhaustive, high-friction inventory.1 The brevity of the single item ensures compliance and reduces survey fatigue, which is a paramount concern in consumer-facing self-tracking technologies.

## **Structural Analysis of Scale Formats: VAS, Likert, and Change Anchors**

If a single-item self-report is deemed viable, the selection of the specific mathematical scale format—Visual Analog Scales (VAS), Likert scales, Numeric Rating Scales (NRS), or descriptive-anchor change scales—materially impacts the sensitivity, reliability, and ultimate validity of the longitudinal trace. The design choice dictates whether the data accurately reflects the athlete's cognitive state or if it introduces artificial step-functions and ceiling effects.

### **Visual Analog Scales (VAS)**

A Visual Analog Scale typically consists of a continuous, unmarked horizontal or vertical line, usually 100 millimeters in physical or digital length, anchored by two extreme verbal descriptors (e.g., "Not at all confident" to "Completely confident"). The primary psychometric advantage of the VAS is its exceptionally high degree of responsiveness and its sensitivity to microscopic intra-individual changes over time.27 In a digital interface, this is typically rendered as a continuous, stepless slider.  
Research on expectancy and self-efficacy measures indicates that VAS formats offer exceptional test-retest reliability. One rigorous methodology study evaluating treatment expectations across participants (N=363) demonstrated that the continuous VAS format yielded a test-retest ICC of 0.856, alongside a significantly higher responsiveness to detect minute changes over time (71%) compared to traditional category scales.29 In broader physical health and psychological state contexts, the VAS consistently demonstrates excellent area under the curve metrics (AUC \= 0.96) for responsiveness 30, with intermethod and intramethod reliabilities reaching an ICC of 0.86.28 In a mobile application context, a continuous slider provides a frictionless, intuitive user experience that forces cognitive engagement; because there are no predefined discrete numbers, it prevents individuals from thoughtlessly tapping the exact same category out of habit, encouraging a true momentary appraisal.

### **Likert and Numeric Rating Scales (NRS)**

Traditional Likert scales (typically utilizing 5-point or 7-point formats) and 11-point Numeric Rating Scales (0 to 10\) represent the most common alternatives to the continuous VAS. While a 5-point Likert scale (e.g., 1 \= Strongly Disagree, 2 \= Disagree, 3 \= Neutral, 4 \= Agree, 5 \= Strongly Agree) offers unparalleled ease of cognitive processing, it suffers from severe ceiling effects and mathematically induced "step-function" plateaus.29 An intermediate adult athlete experiencing slow, incremental progress over a four-week period may not feel that their slight improvement warrants jumping a full 20% from a "3" to a "4" on a 5-point scale. This hesitation leads to artificially flat, unresponsive confidence traces over multiple weeks, effectively destroying the longitudinal utility of the metric.  
By contrast, an 11-point NRS (0 to 10\) offers sufficient mathematical granularity while maintaining the deep cultural familiarity of numerical grading.27 The same expectancy study (N=363) that validated the VAS also evaluated an 8-grade NRS, finding a highly acceptable test-retest ICC of 0.820.29 An 11-point scale mitigates the severe step-functions of the 5-point Likert while remaining highly interpretable for the user.

### **Descriptive-Anchor Change Scales**

Scales that ask for relative, retrospective comparisons—such as "Rate your improvement since last week: Less Confident / Same / More Confident"—introduce entirely distinct cognitive biases that severely compromise construct validity. These scales rely heavily on the fallibility of episodic memory, forcing the user to recall their exact psychological state from seven days prior, compare it to their current state, and compute a mental delta.  
Studies indicate that asking individuals to quantify "improvement" rather than "current capability" conflates perceived past performance outcomes with intrinsic present self-efficacy, degrading the construct validity of the instrument.33 When users are asked to rate relative change, they do not compare their current state to a perfect, objective memory of their past state; rather, they compare their current state to a heuristic simulation of their past state, which is heavily contaminated and biased by their current mood and immediate physical fatigue. Therefore, descriptive-anchor change scales are fundamentally unreliable for high-frequency longitudinal tracking.

| Scale Format | Mathematical Granularity | Sensitivity to Micro-Change | Test-Retest Reliability (ICC) | Recommended Application Use Case |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **5-Point Likert** | Low (20% increments) | Low (Prone to artificial plateaus) | Moderate to High | Broad trait assessments; discrete milestone evaluations |
| **11-Point NRS** | High (10% increments) | High | High (\~0.82) | General digital self-tracking; momentary state appraisal |
| **100-Point VAS** | Very High (Continuous) | Very High | High (\~0.85 \- 0.86) | Longitudinal tracking of micro-fluctuations in state confidence |
| **Change Anchors** | Low (Directional only) | Low (Recall bias heavily impacts) | Highly Variable | Not recommended for weekly state capability tracking |

The psychometric literature strongly supports the use of a 100-point continuous VAS slider or, alternatively, an 11-point NRS for weekly digital self-administration.29 These formats circumvent the step-function plateaus of limited 5-point scales, allow the user to express nuanced micro-adjustments in their weekly state confidence, and possess the requisite mathematical sensitivity to generate a meaningful longitudinal trace.

## **Item Framing: Defensible Formats Versus Collapse Modes**

The specific phrasing and linguistic framing of the single-item self-report dictate whether the metric holds its construct validity or collapses into useless noise. The foundational literature on self-efficacy psychometrics, particularly Bandura’s comprehensive 2006 guide for constructing self-efficacy scales, provides explicit, non-negotiable boundaries for item framing.16 If a digital product deviates from these linguistic guidelines, it ceases to measure self-efficacy and instead measures varying permutations of intention, satisfaction, or delusion.

### **"Can Do" Versus "Will Do" Framing**

Bandura explicitly emphasized that self-efficacy is a judgment of *capability*, not a statement of *intention* or a retrospective evaluation of *past performance*.16 Valid items must be phrased using "can do" or "capability" terminology rather than "will do" phrasing. "Will do" measures a user's behavioral intention, which is conceptually and empirically separable from their actual belief in their physical competence.

* **Invalid Formulation (Intention):** "How confident are you that you will play well next week?" This framing collapses because it measures future intention and outcome expectancy, which are heavily mediated by external factors outside the athlete's control (e.g., opponent skill, weather), rather than current internal capability.  
* **Invalid Formulation (Retrospective):** "Rate your performance in your last session." This framing measures retrospective outcome satisfaction. While related to confidence, it is entirely dominated by recency bias and the specific events of the final ten minutes of practice, destroying its utility as a measure of underlying skill.  
* **Valid Formulation (Capability):** "How confident are you in your capability to execute successfully today?" or "Rate your current confidence in your ability to perform." This framing isolates state capability, aligning perfectly with established psychometric standards.16

### **Absolute State Versus Relative Change**

As previously noted in the scale format analysis, asking users to explicitly rate "improvement since last week" routinely collapses in practice. The most defensible framing evaluates *absolute state confidence right now*. The longitudinal trace—the delta between weeks—should be calculated by the application's backend database, plotting the absolute state scores against the axis of time. The cognitive burden of calculating the derivative of progress must be handled by the software, not by the user's fallible episodic memory.

### **The Necessity of Domain Specificity**

A core tenet of self-efficacy theory is that confidence is largely non-transferable across broad, disparate domains.36 A global, generalized question such as "How confident are you in your overall game right now?" lacks specific construct validity because the user is forced into a complex cognitive averaging exercise. A tennis player must mentally average their highly confident forehand with their highly insecure backhand, resulting in a regression to a generic mean that obscures targeted skill development.  
To maintain validity, the application must prompt the user to rate their confidence in highly specific, granular sub-skills. For example, instead of "overall climbing confidence," the prompt should target "confidence in executing dynamic overhangs," or instead of "overall tennis confidence," it should target "confidence in the topspin serve." Task-specific self-efficacy measures have been shown to account for up to 52% of the variance in actual athletic performance among adult competitors (N=52), whereas global, generalized measures offer vastly inferior predictive power and minimal diagnostic utility.36

## **The Interference of State Confounds: Recency, Mood, and Fatigue**

If a subjective confidence trace is to be successfully utilized as a proxy for actual skill development within a digital product, the degree to which that trace is contaminated by extraneous, non-skill-related state variables must be aggressively quantified. A weekly, single-item measure is profoundly sensitive to ecological and physiological confounds.  
Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) research examining micro-temporal, within-person fluctuations in self-efficacy reveals that perceived capability is not a stable, linear trajectory. A comprehensive scoping review of 13 studies utilizing EMA in physical activity contexts reported that within-person variance for self-efficacy ranges wildly from 51% to 89%.4 This statistical revelation is paramount: it indicates that the overwhelming majority of the variance in any given weekly confidence rating is driven by immediate state-level fluctuations rather than the underlying trait-level acquisition of motor skills.38  
Three primary confounds dominate this variance in the target population of self-coached adult amateurs:

### **Recency Bias and Last-Session Outcomes**

Because self-coached adult amateurs lack objective, third-party coaching feedback to contextualize their performance, they rely almost exclusively on their most recent enactive mastery experience to construct their self-efficacy.18 If an athlete performs poorly or fails a specific drill in the final ten minutes of their third practice session of the week, that acute negative outcome will disproportionately anchor their weekly confidence rating. Recency bias forces the longitudinal trace to reflect the extreme volatility of day-to-day practice outcomes rather than the slow, underlying consolidation of neurological motor learning.7

### **Central Nervous System (CNS) and Peripheral Fatigue**

High training loads, insufficient recovery between the one to three weekly sessions, or general life stressors induce both physical peripheral soreness and Central Nervous System (CNS) fatigue. Research illustrates a powerful, inextricable bidirectional relationship between perceived fatigue, mood, and self-efficacy in athletic populations.6 Individuals reporting higher levels of acute physical fatigue systematically report lower levels of self-confidence and self-efficacy, entirely independent of their actual mechanical skill capacity.6 If an application prompts a user to take a confidence survey while they are experiencing delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS) or acute CNS fatigue, the user will likely report heavily depressed skill confidence, contaminating the data.

### **Transient Mood States**

Negative affect, emotional regulation, and general psychological stress significantly degrade state self-efficacy.7 Cognitive appraisals of physical capability are heavily filtered through the athlete's current emotional state. A stressful day in the workplace preceding a Friday evening practice session will artificially suppress the subsequent self-rated confidence score, creating artificial dips and valleys in the longitudinal trace that have absolutely zero correlation with actual motor skill retention or physical capability.43

### **Architectural Mitigations**

To mitigate these severe confounds, the timing and context of the weekly administration are critical design variables. Administering the confidence prompt immediately at the end of a physical session captures *momentary* confidence, thereby maximizing the interference of acute fatigue, heart rate elevation, and recency bias. Conversely, administering the prompt on a fixed weekly cadence on a designated rest day, or prior to the commencement of the first session of the week, isolates the cognitive appraisal from acute physiological arousal and immediate past-session frustration. This architectural choice yields a much cleaner, more reliable reading of baseline state self-efficacy, minimizing the noise generated by the 51% to 89% within-person variance.

## **Cognitive Biases in Skill Appraisal: Novice Overestimation and Plateau-Blindness**

Perhaps the most compelling argument against taking subjective skill confidence at absolute face value is the fundamentally nonlinear relationship between actual motor skill acquisition and perceived competence. As adult recreational athletes progress from novices (months 0-24) to intermediates (years 2-5), their metacognitive frameworks undergo radical, systematic shifts that systematically distort their self-reported confidence. Understanding these shifts is vital for interpreting the longitudinal trace.

### **The Dunning-Kruger Effect and Novice Overestimation**

The Dunning-Kruger effect posits a dual burden for unskilled individuals: not only do they make erroneous choices and execute tasks poorly, but their specific incompetence robs them of the metacognitive ability to realize it.9 The metacognitive explanation rests on the foundational idea that the exact same skills required to execute a task competently are the skills required to accurately evaluate competence in that domain. Because novices lack these skills, they cannot perceive their own flaws.  
In adult recreational sports, this manifests as extreme novice overestimation. Research surveys suggest that nearly 70% to 75% of unskilled amateur athletes believe they perform above average or believe they can execute highly advanced mechanical maneuvers after minimal exposure.44 A rigorous study examining sport coaches (N=94) demonstrated similar outcomes; when split into quartiles based on objective ability, coaches in the absolute lowest quartile exhibited significantly higher self-efficacy than their objective capabilities warranted, statistically confirming the Dunning-Kruger effect in sport environments.45  
For a self-tracking application, this dynamic means a novice user in their first six months of training will likely input highly inflated baseline confidence scores. Their longitudinal trace will begin artificially high simply because they do not yet possess the domain-specific vocabulary or the perceptual acuity to recognize their gross mechanical flaws.10

### **The Paradox of Progress and Intermediate Plateau-Blindness**

As the amateur athlete transitions into the two-to-five-year intermediate phase—the exact target demographic for this software—their actual motor skills improve, but their metacognitive awareness improves at a highly accelerated rate.48 They begin to deeply understand the vastness of the skill gap between themselves and true experts.  
This triggers a phenomenon recently codified as the "paradox of progress," highlighted in a 2025/2026 randomized controlled trial by Aune investigating structured football interventions for youths (N=105).11 In this landmark study, researchers naturally expected the intervention group to show significantly increased social self-efficacy and perceived competence due to their structured, highly effective skill development. Paradoxically, the *control group* reported significantly higher self-efficacy at the post-test.12 The researchers concluded that the structured sports intervention successfully imparted real skill, but in doing so, it inadvertently heightened the participants' self-awareness of their own limitations.11 The acquisition of actual competence triggered more modest, highly critical, and hyper-accurate self-evaluations.49  
Furthermore, intermediate athletes frequently experience extended motor learning plateaus. During these prolonged phases, underlying neural consolidation and microscopic mechanical efficiencies are occurring, but gross, macro-level performance outcomes appear to stall entirely.15 Because the athlete cannot perceive these microscopic neural gains, and because their metacognition is increasingly critical due to the paradox of progress, their self-rated confidence will likely flatline or drop steeply.14  
This presents a massive diagnostic risk for product design. If a weekly confidence trace dips significantly at week eight of a training block, the application architecture must not automatically interpret this as a failure of the physical training program. It may, in fact, signify a profound metacognitive breakthrough. Plateau-blindness and the paradox of progress dictate that subjective confidence is not a 1:1 topological map of physical skill; rather, it is a map of the athlete's cognitive relationship with their skill. A drop in confidence often signals that the athlete has finally learned enough to know what they are doing wrong.

## **Comparison to Behavioral Proxies**

Given the myriad confounds associated with subjective confidence—ranging from acute fatigue to the Dunning-Kruger effect—an alternative product strategy is to abandon self-reporting entirely and rely exclusively on objective behavioral proxies. In the domain of digital self-tracking and Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), common behavioral proxies include raw session count, longitudinal practice duration, drill-completion rates, and weakest-skill drill volume.52  
Behavioral proxies possess the distinct, unassailable advantage of being entirely immune to mood, fatigue, and metacognitive biases. If an adult recreational athlete completes three structured, high-intensity drill sessions per week for twelve consecutive weeks, the raw volume of practice mathematically dictates a degree of motor learning consolidation and skill acquisition, regardless of whether the athlete subjectively *feels* confident about it.15  
However, replacing subjective confidence entirely with objective behavioral metrics eliminates a crucial, predictive psychological dimension: motivation and persistence.18 Foundational self-efficacy theory demonstrates that subjective confidence is the primary psychological engine driving future behavior.18 Individuals possessing high self-efficacy view difficult tasks as challenges to be mastered, recover rapidly from unexpected setbacks, and exhibit immense persistence when faced with mechanical failure.7 Conversely, if an intermediate athlete's subjective confidence drops precipitously due to plateau-blindness, they are at an exceptionally high risk of churn and program abandonment, even if their behavioral proxies (like session counts) currently look perfectly stable.2  
Furthermore, recent HCI research argues strongly against treating behavioral proxies as the sole indicators of user engagement or capability. In complex studies utilizing eye-tracking, facial affect, and interaction metrics as behavioral proxies (e.g., utilizing AFFDEX attention metrics), researchers found that while objective behavioral data flawlessly captures action, it frequently misses the cognitive load, the emotional frustration, and the experiential reality of the user.53  
Therefore, subjective confidence and behavioral proxies should not be viewed as mutually exclusive competitors vying for a single dashboard slot; they are highly complementary diagnostic tools that must be used in tandem.

* **Divergence Indicates Risk:** If a behavioral proxy is high (the user is diligently completing all assigned drills) but their subjective confidence trace is dropping precipitously, the user is likely experiencing the "paradox of progress" or a frustrating intermediate plateau.11 The application can recognize this divergence and preemptively intervene with educational content normalizing non-linear learning curves, preventing user churn.  
* **Convergence Indicates Mastery:** If both objective drill completion and subjective confidence rise in tandem, the user is experiencing a rare, highly motivating period of conscious competence.

| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Subjective Confidence (VAS/NRS)** | Captures motivation, persistence risk, and internal psychological state.20 | High within-person variance, Dunning-Kruger bias, acute fatigue confounds.4 | Cognitive appraisal of capability; intent for future behavioral adherence.2 |
| **Objective Behavioral Proxy (Drill Count)** | Mathematically objective, entirely immune to mood and cognitive biases.53 | Ignores emotional frustration, burnout risk, and the quality of mechanical execution.54 | Volumetric exposure to the specific motor task.15 |

## **Practitioner Intuition and Commercial App Paradigms**

The theoretical consensus synthesized in the psychometric literature is largely mirrored by the iterative design choices of commercial self-coaching and self-tracking applications. Language learning platforms, music-practice applications, and digital physical therapy programs frequently deploy subjective confidence tracking as a core, user-facing feature, treating it as a vital component of the user experience.56  
A prominent example is the integration of subjective confidence and mastery tracking in language acquisition platforms like Duolingo. Studies analyzing user experience (UX) and self-efficacy in such applications reveal that the gamification of progress tracking fundamentally serves to build learner confidence, particularly for complete beginners.58 However, UX post-mortems and qualitative feedback frequently note that advanced users interact differently with these subjective metrics. While novice users leverage confidence tracking as a primary psychological motivator, advanced users rely more heavily on objective performance metrics, suggesting that the utility of subjective tracking fundamentally shifts as the user climbs the learning curve.58  
In domains like digital speech therapy and social anxiety tracking (e.g., EaseTalk, Unawkward), developers explicitly and deliberately separate "fluency" (the objective behavioral performance) from "confidence" (the subjective capability) within the user interface.56 These platforms operate under the well-founded premise that human progress is not strictly linear; thus, giving users a discrete visual representation of their confidence allows them to view their psychological resilience as a trainable metric completely separate from their physical performance.57 Similarly, the DreamKit application, designed for youth skill tracking, utilizes self-rated mastery explicitly to build broad self-efficacy, recognizing that the act of actively tracking one's progress fundamentally alters and improves the user's psychological relationship to the underlying skill.60  
From a clinical and practitioner standpoint, sport psychologists dealing with amateur adult populations actively encourage the tracking of psychological states alongside physical metrics. Continuous physical activity participation requires managing the dynamic, highly volatile interplay between stress, fatigue, and motivation.61 Practitioners strongly advocate for the use of daily or weekly ecological momentary assessments to help athletes identify the specific environmental and physiological triggers that depress their confidence, enabling them to construct more resilient, sustainable training habits.4

## **Identified Gaps in the Literature**

While the existing literature offers robust, actionable guidance, certain specific phenomena regarding this exact demographic remain under-researched, presenting minor extrapolative risks that product architects should acknowledge:

1. **Longitudinal Data at N \> 100 for Niche Skill-Sports:** While self-efficacy tracking is extensively documented in generic physical activity contexts (e.g., step-counts, basic aerobic exercise, weight loss) 2, there is a notable scarcity of large-sample, long-term longitudinal studies analyzing weekly, digital, single-item confidence traces specifically in self-coached adult recreational athletes engaged in highly technical skill-sports (e.g., bouldering, tennis, golf).42  
2. **Temporal Duration of the Paradox of Progress:** The exact temporal duration of the "plateau-blindness" and the Dunning-Kruger-induced competence dip observed in recent studies (such as Aune, 2025\) is not fully quantified across different sports.11 How many weeks an intermediate athlete typically spends in this state of suppressed, hyper-critical confidence before their objective skill forces a positive subjective re-evaluation remains an open question for future empirical HCI tracking.  
3. **Optimal Debiasing Interventions in UI:** While the confounds of fatigue and mood are exceptionally well documented, the specific UX interventions required to debias a user *before* they enter a subjective rating (e.g., prompting them to explicitly ignore their sore muscles before answering) lack deep, peer-reviewed A/B testing in commercial environments.

## **Conclusion**

The core research question posed—whether a single-item weekly self-rated skill confidence reading carries usable construct validity as a longitudinal skill-development proxy for adult recreational athletes—can be answered in the affirmative, provided the application architecture explicitly accounts for the metric's inherent volatility and psychological complexity.  
Weekly subjective confidence does not, and cannot, measure the absolute, objective aggregation of neurological motor pathways. Rather, it measures state self-efficacy, a psychological construct heavily dominated by recency bias, transient mood, physical fatigue, and shifting metacognitive biases. For the target population of self-coached intermediate athletes (two to five years of experience), actual motor skill acquisition will inevitably trigger the Dunning-Kruger "paradox of progress," wherein the acquisition of true competence strips away novice illusions, leading to hyper-critical self-evaluations and plateau-blindness. If the application's logic interprets a dip in confidence purely as a degradation in physical skill, it will fundamentally misdiagnose the athlete's progress.  
However, abandoning subjective confidence entirely in favor of purely objective behavioral proxies would eliminate the most critical predictor of long-term adherence, motivation, and psychological resilience. Self-efficacy is the engine of persistence. The optimal architectural solution is to implement the subjective confidence trace using a 100-point continuous Visual Analog Scale (VAS) or an 11-point Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) to ensure mathematical sensitivity. The item must be framed precisely as a measure of present capability (e.g., "How confident are you in your capability to execute successfully today?"), administered on a fixed weekly cadence distinctly removed from the immediate visceral aftermath of a demanding training session.  
By pairing this highly sensitive, behaviorally predictive subjective trace with an objective behavioral proxy like drill completion volume, the application can accurately triangulate both the psychological resilience and the physical progression of the adult recreational athlete, transforming raw data into a deeply motivating, context-aware user experience.
