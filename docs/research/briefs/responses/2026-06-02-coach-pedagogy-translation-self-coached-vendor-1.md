# Translation of coach-presence pedagogy techniques to self-administered training apps

## Executive summary

For **Technique A — athlete-set per-session goal aligned to the practice focus**, the best verdict is **partially translates under specific framings**, with **moderate confidence**. The strongest sport-specific synthesis still shows goal setting helps performance overall, but the effect is smaller in adults and more experienced performers than in younger or novice samples, and benefits are stronger when the goal is structured and supported rather than left as a fully self-generated ritual. Mechanism studies also show that process goals plus self-monitoring can improve motor learning and self-efficacy without a coach actively reviewing the goal, which means coach presence is not the only active ingredient. But digital behavior-change work points the same way as the sport literature: fully self-set goals are harder to sustain than assigned or adaptively suggested goals, and unstructured tracking can turn into friction, guilt, or abandonment. The product implication is to **ship a lightweight, process-goal version**, not a blank “write your goal” field. The safest build is a short choose-or-edit prompt tied to the session focus, followed by a brief end-of-session check on whether the cue was used. citeturn3search14turn5view0turn12view0turn16view1turn18view0turn23view1turn23view2

For **Technique B — forward-looking outcome promise**, the best verdict is also **partially translates under specific framings**, but with **low-to-moderate confidence**. The “does anything survive without a coach?” answer is yes: sport placebo research and motor-learning expectancy work show that simple expectancy manipulations can improve performance and learning even when the intervention is little more than a brief suggestion, and a meta-analysis of enhanced-expectancy studies reports a moderate average benefit, albeit with serious small-study and publication-bias concerns. That said, the direct **app-mediated** evidence is much thinner. The cleanest adjacent self-administered analogue I found is a smartphone-delivered relaxation study where a positive expectancy intervention improved exercise completion but did **not** improve felt relaxation, increased dropout, and produced explicitly negative reactions in some users. So the likely translation is not “motivational promise works as-is”; it is “a narrow, plausible expectancy cue can help, but broad confidence promises are risky.” The product implication is to **ship only a proximal, truthful, non-guaranteed version** of this surface. citeturn28search11turn31search0turn34view0turn41view0turn45view0

For the **pair together**, the evidence favors **supportive but only when the loop is extremely lean**. There is no direct self-coached adult-skill-sport study of “goal capture + expectancy statement + closure reflection” as a package. But lab evidence shows additive benefits when expectancy support is combined with autonomy-supportive design, and that combination is conceptually close to your proposed pair: Technique A supplies agency and attentional targeting; Technique B supplies competence expectancy. The practical recommendation is to **treat the pair as one motivational frame, not two separate chores**. One brief user-set process cue plus one short, honest expectancy sentence is defensible; two heavy start-of-session surfaces are not. citeturn47view0turn48view0turn18view0

## Evidence standard and mechanism lens

The main limitation in this evidence base is not whether coached sport literature exists. It does. The problem is **transportability**: most direct sport experiments are in youth, students, older adults in lab tasks, or coached settings, while the target use case here is adult recreational, self-coached, skill-sport practice one to three times per week. I therefore weighted evidence in this order: direct self-administered sport or exercise studies first; mechanism studies that isolate expectancy, goal structure, or self-monitoring second; adjacent digital-health and self-tracking studies third; and practitioner-oriented theory only as a supplement. That weighting matters because the central design question is not “do goals and expectancies ever help?” but “what survives when the coach disappears?” citeturn3search14turn5view0turn32view0turn45view0

The strongest mechanism-level takeaway across both techniques is that **interpersonal coach presence is an amplifier, not the sole active ingredient**. Goal-setting benefits do not collapse to zero when a coach is absent; process goals and self-recording can still improve learning. Expectancy effects also do not require a rich coach-athlete relationship; quite minimal suggestions can change performance. But the same literature also shows that **structure, competence support, and believable framing matter a great deal**, and that poorly designed self-monitoring or hollow motivational language can produce avoidance, rumination, or attrition. In other words: the app can substitute for some of the cognitive mechanism, but it cannot get away with a weak imitation of coach theater. citeturn12view0turn34view0turn45view0turn23view1turn23view2

## Technique A

The coached-context case for Technique A is real but not blanket-generalizable. The most useful recent synthesis is Williamson and colleagues’ meta-analysis of goal setting in sport, which covered **27 studies and 100 effect sizes** and found an overall positive effect on sport performance of about **Hedges g = 0.50**. That is not trivial. But the same review found the effect was **smaller in adults** and **smaller in more experienced performers** than in younger and less experienced samples, and it also reported that who sets the goal matters: more externally structured goal-setting conditions outperformed looser self-set arrangements. That is exactly the pattern a product team should care about. Your users are not novices walking into a coach-led program; they are adults training themselves, and that is the part of the literature where the “just write a goal” move looks least robust. citeturn3search14turn5view0

The mechanism evidence, however, is enough to reject the claim that Technique A is only social-accountability theater. Zimmerman and Kitsantas’ motor-learning studies showed that **process goals plus self-monitoring/self-evaluation** improved dart-throwing skill, self-efficacy, self-reactions, and intrinsic interest in learners practicing without a real coaching loop. In the 1997 paper, **90 high-school girls** were randomized across process-goal versus outcome-goal and self-evaluation conditions; students who focused on process goals and self-evaluation made more strategic attributions and showed better motivational and learning outcomes than those focused on outcome goals without self-evaluation. The important product translation is that the active ingredient is not “goal declaration by itself.” It is **a process cue that narrows attention plus a reflective loop that makes the cue usable**. citeturn12view0

Adjacent digital evidence points in the same direction. In a fully automated smartphone RCT, Zhou and colleagues randomized **64 adults** to adaptive personalized daily step goals versus a fixed 10,000-step target; by 10 weeks the personalized-goal arm had a **net advantage of 960 steps per day** over control. The same paper explicitly notes that digital interventions often find **assigned or personalized goals outperform fully self-set goals**, because self-set input is harder to maintain over time. In Nuijten and colleagues’ 8-week gamified mHealth trial, **176 participants** entered a self-guided physical-activity campaign; participants who completed the intake survey and set a goal were more engaged, visiting the app on **1.625 to 2.176 more days per wave** than those with no goal, and the personalization that mattered most was not intensity inflation but **frequency framing** for sports sessions. Separately, Gordon and colleagues’ large observational MyFitnessPal study of **1.4 million users** shows that self-set goals can anchor behavior in the wild, but also that success strongly depends on early self-monitoring patterns rather than the mere presence of a goal artifact. Taken together, that is enough to say the self-administered version can work, but the design must do more than ask for an aspiration and move on. citeturn16view1turn18view0turn19view0turn19view3turn21view0

The failure mode is equally clear. Self-tracking and self-monitoring can become burdensome, especially when the user repeatedly confronts evidence of nonattainment without an interpretable adjustment path. Attig and Franke’s attrition study of **159 former tracker users** found that abandonment clustered around loss of tracking motivation, perceived useless or inaccurate data, and negative attitudes toward personal quantification. Eikey and colleagues’ rumination analysis synthesizes a darker side of self-tracking: users can slide from reflection into repetitive negative self-evaluation, guilt, and even technology abandonment. That is the core risk of turning per-session goal capture into a compulsory blank field. The recommended framing is therefore **not** “What is your goal today?” in the abstract. It is something like: **“Pick one thing to focus on in this session”** followed by two to four process-level options matched to the chosen skill, with optional edit, and a simple end-of-session closure such as **“Did you use this cue?”** or **“What changed when you used it?”**. That recommendation is an inference from the process-goal, self-monitoring, and adaptive-goal evidence, not a direct verbatim copy from one study. citeturn23view1turn23view2turn16view1turn18view0turn12view0

My build advice for Technique A is therefore blunt: **ship it, but only in structured process-goal form**. Do not ship a free-text, self-evaluative, “prove something today” surface. Do not require users to author a novel goal every session. Use constrained process cues, make skipping easy, and always close the loop at session end. If you cannot afford the end-of-session closure, the case for the start-of-session capture weakens materially. citeturn12view0turn16view1turn23view1

## Technique B

Technique B has a stronger pure-mechanism story and a weaker direct-app story. On mechanism, the literature is clear that expectancy can affect performance without needing a dense interpersonal coaching relationship. Hurst and colleagues’ systematic review of placebo and nocebo effects in sport performance pooled **32 studies with 1,513 participants** and found an overall effect of about **Cohen’s d = 0.38**, with similarly small-to-moderate effects across placebo and nocebo manipulations. Bacelar and colleagues’ later meta-analysis of enhanced expectancies in motor learning included **48 studies and 56 effect sizes** and estimated **Hedges g = 0.54**, while also arguing that this effect is probably inflated by underpowered studies and reporting bias. The correct read is not “expectancy is fake” and not “expectancy is magic.” It is “expectancy is real, moderate in the lab, and likely smaller in production.” citeturn28search11turn31search0turn31search2

Specific experiments make the translation point sharper. McKay, Lewthwaite, and Wulf tested a very lean expectancy manipulation in **31 participants** performing a throwing task: after a first block, the experimental group was told they were especially likely to do well under pressure, while controls received neutral information. The expectancy group improved throwing accuracy in the subsequent pressured block; controls did not. Wulf and Lewthwaite’s work on “conceptions of ability” makes a related point: framing a task as a **learnable skill** rather than a test of fixed inherent ability improves self-efficacy, automaticity, and learning. And Wulf, Chiviacowsky, and Lewthwaite’s older-adult study found that even a simple opening statement that peers usually do well on a balance task increased self-efficacy and learning in older women. Those findings matter because they show the coach’s embodied presence is **not** necessary for expectancy to influence performance. A sentence can be enough. citeturn34view0turn34view1turn41view0

But direct self-administered app evidence is thin and cautionary. The most relevant analogue I found is Schienle and Unger’s randomized trial of progressive muscle relaxation delivered through a smartphone app. **160 psychology students** were assigned to a 14-day PMR course with or without an open-label placebo expectancy intervention. The expectancy intervention included an explanation of placebo effects and the suggestion that the placebo would activate the body’s natural relaxation response. The result was mixed: the expectancy arm completed **more exercises on average** (**9.75** vs **8.15**), but the groups did **not** differ in reported relaxation gains; the expectancy arm also had **higher dropout**, and some participants described the intervention negatively, including reactions like feeling foolish or annoyed. That is directly relevant to your technique. App-mediated promises can lift completion a bit while simultaneously failing to improve experienced value and risking trust damage if users perceive the message as contrived. citeturn45view0

That makes the framing boundary decisive. The defensible version of Technique B is **proximal, conditional, and phenomenological**. Good forms are closer to: **“If you stay with this session, you should finish with a clearer feel for clean contact on this skill,”** or **“Expect one small gain today: either a few cleaner reps or a better sense of what to adjust next time.”** Those formulations are narrow enough to be believable and close enough to the practice task to piggyback on expectancy and attentional-priming mechanisms. The risky forms are global or trait-like: **“By the end of practice, you will be better at passing”** or repeated claims that users will feel confident every time. The lab literature supports expectancy enhancement; the app analogue shows that **overclaiming is the real failure mode**. This recommendation is partly inferential, but it is tightly anchored to the expectancy, placebo, and honesty/trust findings above. citeturn28search11turn31search0turn34view0turn45view0

So the product verdict for Technique B is: **ship only a narrow expectancy cue, not a motivational promise**. Use it as a short orientation line, not as inspirational copy. Make it about what the user is likely to notice or leave with after doing the work, not about identity change or guaranteed confidence. If the copy team insists on “confidence,” the safest version is a hedge such as **“you may finish with a clearer sense of what confident execution feels like”** rather than **“you should feel more confident.”** citeturn34view1turn45view0

## Pair compounding and practitioner intuition

The direct literature on your exact pair is basically absent, so the best available evidence is the motivational-combination work from motor learning. Wulf, Chiviacowsky, and Cardozo tested autonomy support and enhanced expectancies in a factorial motor-learning design with **64 high-school students**. Each factor independently improved retention and transfer performance, with retention **η²p = .062** for both autonomy support and expectancy, and transfer **η²p = .105** for autonomy support and **.081** for expectancy. The combined group performed best, and the authors reported an interpretable practical gap: averaged across retention and transfer, control accuracy was **20.7**, autonomy support alone **31.2**, expectancy alone **30.7**, and the combined condition **38.8**. That supports a **compound rather than competitive** reading when the two surfaces target different psychological needs. citeturn47view0turn48view0

That said, the practitioner lesson is not “stack more motivation widgets.” It is “support agency and competence with almost no overhead.” In practice, Technique A should do the autonomy/attentional work and Technique B should do the expectancy work. The moment both surfaces become wordy, evaluative, or visibly ceremonial, they start competing for the same thin pool of pre-session attention. The pair is most defensible when A is one quick user action and B is one short line of honest orientation. The post-session reflection should close the loop on **A first**; B should be treated as context, not as another thing the user has to grade every time. citeturn48view0turn12view0turn45view0

On substitutes for social accountability, the broader digital literature suggests that apps regain some lost mechanism when they add social comparison, partner visibility, or programmatic scaffolds rather than relying on private self-talk alone. Nuijten’s trial used leaderboards and found engagement gains were shaped by goal-setting and personalization, not just the existence of tasks. But for a solo, quick-start, local-first training app, the cleanest substitute is not a faux coach voice. It is a **structured closing surface** that asks whether the user used the chosen cue and what changed. That is the lightest way to recreate the “someone will ask me about this later” function without pretending the app is a person. citeturn18view0turn19view0turn12view0

## Gaps and decisive next study

The biggest unresolved point is not whether goal setting and expectancy work in principle. It is **effect size erosion in your exact population**. The direct evidence base for self-coached adult recreational skill-sport users is still thin. Technique A has enough adjacent support to justify a constrained ship. Technique B has enough mechanism evidence to reject a blanket “does not translate” call, but not enough production-context evidence to justify bold copy. The practical uncertainty is therefore about **how much lift survives in a low-frequency, adult, real-world app context once lab-style scaffolding disappears**. citeturn3search14turn16view1turn45view0

The study that would most change the answer is a **factorial field RCT in the shipped app**. Recruit adult recreational skill-sport users with roughly the target profile, randomize them at session start to **A on/off × B on/off**, and keep the session core constant. For A, compare a structured process-goal picker against no goal surface. For B, compare a proximal expectancy line against a neutral session description. Pre-register four primary outcomes: session-start-to-first-drill completion, session completion, post-session perceived value, and 7-day return. Add secondary outcomes for self-efficacy delta, remembered session focus, and 8-week adherence. Because the likely production effects are small, I would size for a **small effect range** rather than a lab-sized one; in practice that means **roughly 400–500 total participants** rather than a small academic sample. That sample-size recommendation is an inference from the pattern already in the literature: moderate lab effects, smaller adult/experienced effects, and mixed app outcomes. citeturn3search14turn31search0turn45view0

If that study shows A improves completion or next-week return with negligible startup friction, you should keep it. If B improves post-session value only when phrased narrowly and hurts trust under stronger wording, that resolves the copy question cleanly. If the interaction is positive, ship the pair as one lightweight frame. If the interaction is null and startup friction rises, keep A and drop B. Right now, that is the most likely decision boundary. citeturn48view0turn45view0
